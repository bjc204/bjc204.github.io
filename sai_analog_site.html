Stratospheric aerosol injection · natural analogs

Multiple lines of evidence suggest modest but positive radiative effects on vegetation from stratospheric aerosol injection

Brendan Clark (Cornell University) · Ben Kravitz (Indiana University) · Daniele Visioni (Cornell University) · Manuel Lerdau (University of Virginia)

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) to limit global warming overshoot would reduce total solar radiation while increasing the proportion of diffuse light reaching Earth's surface, creating competing effects on plant growth. Analog studies using volcanic eruptions show impacts ranging from significantly negative to strongly positive (Proctor et al., 2018; Gu et al., 2003), and numerical models suggest radiative effects from SAI would range from neutral to slightly positive (Xia et al., 2016; Fan et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2023).

Here we show, using eddy covariance measurements at eight AmeriFlux sites (four maize and four forest), that radiative effects of smoke are almost always positive. We attribute inconsistencies in the literature to limitations in the underlying assumptions and methods of previous analog studies; addressing these issues brings their results into agreement with our own, and with numerical models.

1 · The problem

One volcanic analog, two opposite answers

Global numerical modeling studies have indicated that the counteracting effects of radiation from SAI would tend to have an overall net effect on crops and natural vegetation ranging from neutral to slightly positive. However, studies using natural analogs for SAI to confirm or disprove such modeling studies have so far been limited, and the two best known reach opposite conclusions:

Both studies use the same kind of natural experiment, a large eruption that dims and diffuses sunlight, yet one finds a large gain and the other a significant loss. This site follows how we tried to resolve that discrepancy: first by adding a new, well-observed analog, wildfire smoke, and then by revisiting the methods of both earlier studies.

2 · A new analog

Wildfire smoke dims and diffuses sunlight much like volcanic sulfate

Here we utilize eddy covariance measurements at four cropland and four forested AmeriFlux sites to understand how changes in radiation during wildfire smoke events impact GPP. Wildfire smoke events, similar to the sulfate aerosols emitted during large volcanic eruptions and proposed for SAI, increase the proportion of diffuse radiation reaching the surface while simultaneously reducing total incoming solar radiation. They provide a more recent analog for SAI, offering additional observational data to better understand its potential radiative effects.

Smoke-induced light changes raise GPP at every site

Across the eight AmeriFlux sites, wildfire smoke-induced radiation perturbations never decreased GPP, with seven of the eight sites exhibiting significantly positive GPP responses. Wildfire smoke also tends to lower air temperature and VPD by reducing the solar radiation reaching the surface, which increased GPP at many of the sites. However, we cannot separate these changes from those caused by the weather conditions that accompany smoke events. Soil moisture is not significantly affected by wildfire smoke at any sites. The total effect of smoke increases overall GPP by 4.6 to 18.9% for maize sites, and by 1.5 to 18.3% for forest sites.

3 · Is smoke a fair stand-in?

For a given aerosol load, sulfate diffuses more light than smoke

We used the libRadtran radiative transfer model to quantify how additional aerosols alter the amount and composition of sunlight reaching the surface, comparing wildfire smoke with stratospheric sulfate aerosols, the proposed aerosol for SAI. Smoke optical properties were taken as the median AERONET retrievals across all smoke time steps at the eight sites (single-scattering albedo 0.96, asymmetry parameter 0.65, Ångström exponent 1.69). Stratospheric sulfate was represented by a lognormal size distribution with an effective radius of 0.4 µm, characteristic of the aerosol produced by the 1991 Mt. Pinatubo eruption, and treated as non-absorbing.

libRadtran tends to overestimate the fraction of diffuse radiation from wildfire smoke. For a given AOD, the effects of SAI with sulfate would increase GPP more than smoke.

4 · Revisiting Gu et al. (2003)

The 23% gain at Harvard Forest comes from how the model was fitted

Gu et al. (2003) use a regression model that cannot separate changes in weather from correlated changes in radiation and fit its parameters separately for each year to clear-sky Net Ecosystem Exchange (NEE) at Harvard Forest, using radiation measured at Albany. While there are structural differences between Gu et al. (2003) and our regression model, we show that the gap between our estimates comes from how the models are fitted. 5 · Revisiting Proctor et al. (2018)

Area weighting and the 1992 southern African drought remove the maize loss

Proctor et al. (2018) also use an ordinary least squares regression, like our analysis, fitting data from 1980–2005. However, their regression is on country-level data and applies no area weighting, so the estimated response is the average response of a country in their sample, regardless of how much of the crop it grows. Weighting each country by its maize harvested area changes the estimated negative effect of reduced insolation from volcanic eruptions on maize yield from −5.4% to −3.0%, and the 95% confidence interval of the weighted estimate includes zero. Area weighting also allows a better comparison to crop model simulations, as they typically operate under a fixed map of harvested area (Jägermeyr et al., 2021).

Additionally, the regression model used by Proctor et al. (2018) relies on only a few years after eruptions (1982–83 for El Chichón and 1992 for Mount Pinatubo) to estimate the insolation effect of aerosols. The anomalously large 1992 drought in southern Africa is largely unexplained by the controls in Proctor et al.'s model (temperature, precipitation, cloud cover and ENSO). This drought was caused by a strong El Niño event, not by Pinatubo (Ibebuchi, 2021).

Maize yield changes not explained by Proctor et al.'s (2018) controls (temperature, precipitation, cloud cover and ENSO), averaged over southern Africa (South Africa, Zimbabwe, Zambia, Malawi, Mozambique, Botswana, Namibia, Lesotho and Eswatini) and the rest of the world, 1988–1996. (Manuscript Fig. 4b.)

Maize yields there were about 40% lower than the controls predict, while yields in the rest of the world were close to expected. Because this drought coincides with the year following Pinatubo, much of the loss is erroneously attributed to insolation. Combined with area weighting, this causes the insolation effect to be zero, not negative. For the other crops (rice, soybean, and wheat), area weighting causes the insolation effect to be indistinct from zero.

6 · Applying the smoke relationships to SAI

Under SAI, more diffuse light outweighs the loss of total light

We drove each site's regression with output from the Community Earth System Model version 2 (CESM2-WACCM6) for the G6-1.5K-SAI scenario, which injects SO₂ in equal amounts at 30°N and 30°S to hold global mean temperature at 1.5 K above preindustrial under SSP2-4.5 (Lee et al., 2026). For each site and calendar month over 2065–2084, the CESM2-simulated changes in light and diffuse fraction were applied to that site's tower observations.

At six sites, the benefit from enhanced diffuse radiation outweighs the reduction in GPP from less total radiation, with two sites showing the net effect to be zero. Overall, most sites show an increase to GPP under SAI of about 2% due to more favorable radiation. Two out of the four maize sites suggest no effect of radiation from SAI on GPP, consistent with the revised Proctor et al. (2018) results.

7 · Why a few percent matters

Small GPP changes can move the land carbon sink a lot

While these increases seem modest, small changes in GPP (propagated over the entire globe) can lead to large increases in the land carbon sink because the land sink (NEE) is only a small fraction of two large fluxes, photosynthesis and ecosystem respiration (Mercado et al., 2009). In Mercado et al. (2009), an increase in the land carbon sink of 24% driven by air pollution came from a 1% increase in GPP. This suggests that the modest GPP increase projected under SAI (about +2%), if it held globally, could substantially increase the land carbon sink. However, this enhancement may weaken over time as respiration from the enlarged vegetation and soil carbon pools catches up with the increased photosynthetic uptake.

Where the evidence now sits

We use wildfire smoke as a natural analog for SAI, demonstrating that its radiative effects are similar to those of sulfate aerosols, resulting in an increase of GPP. Our analysis suggests radiative effects from SAI would range from neutral to slightly positive, with potentially large increases in the land carbon sink. Correcting limitations in previous studies aligns both modeling and observational evidence.

Multiple lines of evidence from wildfire smoke, volcanic eruptions, air pollution, and numerical modeling now broadly converge on the conclusion that aerosols similar to those of SAI would tend to have a positive radiative effect on plant photosynthesis.

8 · Data and code

Data and code

All the processed data and code to replicate the findings here are available at github.com/bjc204/SAI_analogs. Eddy covariance data are from the AmeriFlux data portal; aerosol data from NASA AERONET; smoke polygons from NOAA HMS; canopy greenness from the PhenoCam Network; the Proctor et al. (2018) replication data from Zenodo; and CESM2-WACCM6 simulations from Lee et al. (2026).
